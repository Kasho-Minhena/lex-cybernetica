# Reader's Note

Law is one of civilization's oldest technologies. It assigns status, distributes authority, records obligations, and decides whose account of an event becomes official. Cybernetic integration does not make those functions obsolete. It makes their hidden assumptions visible.

Lex-Cybernetica begins from a simple proposition: dignity cannot rationally depend on the material from which a mind is made. A biological person does not become less entitled to liberty by accepting a neural prosthesis. A synthetic consciousness does not become property merely because it executes on infrastructure owned by another. At the same time, recognition cannot rest on aspiration alone. Rights, duties, continuity, control, and evidence require institutions capable of making difficult distinctions.

This treatise therefore rejects two easy answers. It rejects biological supremacy, which treats augmentation as degradation and synthetic consciousness as chattel. It also rejects technological absolutism, which assumes that computational capacity dissolves responsibility, sovereignty, or the need for due process.

The Substrate Integration Index is the book's principal regulatory instrument. It classifies functional dependence and technical risk; it does not measure intelligence, citizenship, or moral worth. The remaining volumes build outward from that distinction.

The judicial decisions in Volume IV are deliberately imagined. They operate as legal thought experiments: each case places one or more statutory premises under pressure and demonstrates how a court might reconcile competing rights.

This first edition is intended to stand on its own as a work of speculative jurisprudence and to serve as the constitutional foundation for the accompanying fiction series.
