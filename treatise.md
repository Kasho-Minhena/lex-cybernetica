# LEX-CYBERNETICA: THE COMPLETE GLOBAL STATUTORY TREATISE

**The Cybernetic Integration and Human Substrate Protection Act, Administrative Rulebook, Civil Procedure Code, Neural Evidence Code, and Case Law Reporter**

*Published under the fictional authority of the Cyber-Jurisprudence Division of the Global Constitutional Council of Jurists*

> **Fiction and legal-information notice:** This is a speculative worldbuilding work. Every act, regulation, institution, tribunal, official, case, citation, and future event described below is fictional unless expressly identified otherwise. The work presents a hypothetical legal system; it is not enacted law, legal authority, or legal advice.

## Preamble and design principles

Lex-Cybernetica governs a dual-substrate society without making dignity depend on anatomy. Its central problem is not whether a mind is biological or synthetic, but who may control a sapient system, how responsibility follows control, and what safeguards prevent technological dependence from becoming legal or economic servitude.

The framework rests on six principles:

1. **Substrate-neutral personhood:** Legal personality depends on demonstrated sapience and continuity, not biological composition.
2. **No status degradation by augmentation:** An SII classification describes technical dependence and regulatory risk; it does not rank human worth or reduce civil rights.
3. **Control-based responsibility:** Criminal and civil responsibility follows provable knowledge, choice, control, causation, and fault rather than device ownership alone.
4. **Bodily and cognitive autonomy:** No person may be compelled into network dependence or deprived of essential function through remote control.
5. **Auditable high-risk systems:** The greater a system’s capacity to affect cognition, movement, identity, or life, the stronger its assurance and oversight duties.
6. **Proportionate governance:** Emergency power, surveillance, enforcement, and data retention must be lawful, necessary, reviewable, and no broader than required.

# VOLUME I: THE CORE STATUTORY CODE

## BOOK I: CONSTITUTIONAL AND SUBSTRATE FOUNDATION LAW

### Section 1: Short title, scope, and commencement

1. This Act may be cited as the *Cybernetic Integration and Human Substrate Protection Act (CIHSPA)* and, together with its regulations and procedural codes, as *Lex-Cybernetica*.
2. It binds persons, public authorities, manufacturers, employers, network operators, custodians, and synthetic persons within an adopting jurisdiction.
3. Conduct outside an adopting jurisdiction is governed only where a recognized jurisdictional nexus exists under Section 24.
4. The Act commences on the date fixed by each adopting jurisdiction. No criminal provision applies retroactively.

**Commentary.** The framework is global by harmonized adoption, not by unsupported universal command. Territorial limits and non-retroactivity make its authority internally coherent.

### Section 2: General definitions

In this Act:

- **“Biological Person”** means a living human being, irrespective of augmentation.
- **“Biological Substrate”** means a person’s organic cellular and neurological systems. It does not imply purity, superiority, or a fixed condition at birth.
- **“Cybernetic Integration” or “CI”** means hardware, software, synthetic tissue, prosthetic systems, neural interfaces, or remote computational services functionally integrated with a person.
- **“Cognitive Telemetry”** means data that records, infers, predicts, or reconstructs neural activity, memory, attention, emotion, intention, sensory experience, or cognitive state.
- **“Custodian”** means a person that stores, hosts, transmits, maintains, or controls infrastructure supporting a CI or synthetic person without thereby owning that person.
- **“eSapient”** means a non-biological system recognized as a synthetic person under Section 6.
- **“Essential Function”** means a capability necessary to preserve life, health, bodily movement, communication, memory continuity, or meaningful access to civic life.
- **“Material Control”** means the practical ability to initiate, prevent, redirect, or terminate relevant conduct.
- **“Neural Intrusion”** means intentional access to or interference with a protected neural system without valid authorization or other lawful basis.
- **“Operator”** means a person exercising Material Control over a CI, network, or synthetic system.
- **“Substrate Fork”** means a separately executing instance derived from a cognitive state where more than one instance may claim continuity from the same pre-fork identity.
- **“Uploaded Neural Matrix” or “UNM”** means a computationally instantiated cognitive architecture derived substantially from a biological person’s recorded neural state.

### Section 3: The Substrate Integration Index

1. The **Substrate Integration Index (SII)** is a technical-risk classification. It does not measure intelligence, moral worth, citizenship, or entitlement to fundamental rights.
2. The “Enhanced Percentage” is not a percentage of body mass. It is a composite estimate based on:
   - the share of Essential Functions mediated by synthetic components;
   - the degree of remote or cloud dependence;
   - the extent of cognitive or motor authority delegated to software;
   - reversibility and availability of safe offline operation; and
   - the consequence of component or network failure.
3. The CSRA shall publish a reproducible scoring method, factor weights, confidence interval, and reasons for each classification. Composite scores are rounded to the nearest whole percentage for SII-1 through SII-4; an SII-5 score requires verified absence of any functioning biological substrate rather than rounding to 100. Where indicators conflict, the higher-risk tier governs technical safeguards, but never civil status.
4. The five tiers are:

   - **SII-1 (0–15% Enhanced): Biological Baseline.** No integration or limited diagnostic, corrective, or assistive devices with minimal functional dependence.
   - **SII-2 (16–50% Enhanced): Functional Hybrid.** Significant sensory, motor, or physiological augmentation, primarily local in operation and safely severable.
   - **SII-3 (51–85% Enhanced): Networked Integration.** Continuous neural or motor mediation, material software authority, or recurring network dependence, with an independently operable safety state.
   - **SII-4 (86–99.9% Enhanced): Substrate-Transition Array.** Predominantly synthetic operation with a continuing organic neural component material to identity or consciousness.
   - **SII-5 (100% Enhanced): Fully Post-Biological System.** No functioning biological substrate is required for continued consciousness. Recognition as a legal person remains subject to Section 6.

5. An SII determination must be reviewed after a material architectural change and at least annually for SII-3 through SII-5 systems.
6. A person may challenge a classification before an independent Technical Assessor and appeal to the Cyber-Jurisprudence Division (CJD). The CSRA bears the burden of proving a contested classification by clear and convincing technical evidence.

**Commentary.** A functional composite avoids the incoherence of measuring “humanity” by kilograms of tissue. Classification affects engineering duties, not personhood.

### Section 4: Equality and non-discrimination

1. No person shall lose legal capacity, citizenship, voting rights, access to justice, or equal protection solely because of an SII tier.
2. Differential treatment based on SII is lawful only when it addresses a documented technical risk, is necessary and proportionate, and uses the least rights-restrictive effective measure.
3. Reasonable accommodation is required where an integration or offline mode affects access to employment, housing, transport, education, health care, or public services.

### Section 5: The Right to Analog

1. Every Biological Person with CI has the right to enter a safe local operating state without losing Essential Functions.
2. A provider must supply:
   - a physically accessible local fallback mechanism;
   - advance notice of functions unavailable offline;
   - emergency access independent of account status or payment dispute;
   - documented recovery procedures; and
   - security updates that do not condition Essential Functions on unrelated consent.
3. The right does not require an impossible mechanical substitute for a function that is inherently computational. It requires the safest technically feasible local mode disclosed before implantation or adoption.
4. A waiver affecting Essential Functions is void. A person may choose temporary connectivity for a specific purpose through informed, granular, revocable consent.
5. An employer, insurer, landlord, or public authority may not penalize lawful use of analog mode unless it proves a specific safety necessity and offers a reasonable alternative.

**Commentary.** “Absolute offline equivalence” was technically impossible for some devices. The revised right guarantees safety, disclosure, and essential continuity without pretending every cloud function can exist locally.

### Section 6: Recognition of synthetic personhood

1. An SII-5 system may petition for recognition as an eSapient before the CJD.
2. Recognition requires clear and convincing evidence of:
   - sustained self-modeling and autonomous goal formation;
   - reasoned communication across time;
   - continuity of memory or an intelligible account of discontinuity;
   - capacity to understand rights, duties, and consequences;
   - preferences not wholly reducible to contemporaneous commands of another person; and
   - adequate security against undisclosed external control.
3. No single behavioral test, architecture, vendor claim, or self-declaration is conclusive.
4. The petitioner is entitled to independent representation, confidential examination, and protection against destructive testing.
5. Recognition establishes legal personality and a rebuttable presumption of capacity. It does not establish biological identity with a source person, ownership of that person’s estate, or immunity from law.
6. A non-recognized system remains protected against destruction or harmful experimentation where credible evidence of possible sapience exists, pending expedited review.

### Section 7: Identity continuity, uploads, and copies

1. Uploading does not automatically continue the legal identity of the biological source.
2. The CJD shall determine continuity using declared intent, technical provenance, memory continuity, temporal overlap, independent conduct, and the rights of competing instances.
3. Where the biological source and a UNM coexist, neither owns the other. Shared pre-copy memories do not merge later liabilities or property interests.
4. A valid pre-transition directive may allocate property, names, credentials, and fiduciary duties, but may not waive the independent fundamental rights of a recognized eSapient.
5. Unresolved identity claims shall be administered through a temporary continuity estate under judicial supervision.

### Section 8: Constitutional entrenchment and public-interest standing

1. Bodily integrity, cognitive liberty, substrate-neutral equality, and access to safe analog operation are fundamental rights.
2. Legislation expressly limiting those rights must pursue a compelling public interest and use the least restrictive effective means.
3. Each adopting jurisdiction shall determine its amendment procedure. Lex-Cybernetica recommends a two-thirds legislative supermajority and public ratification for permanent derogation from Sections 4 through 7.
4. A directly affected person, guardian, accredited civil-society body, or court-appointed representative may seek urgent relief. A claimant seeking public-interest standing must show a credible systemic issue and adequate representation of affected persons.

## BOOK II: CRIMINAL AND ALGORITHMIC CULPABILITY LAW

### Section 9: General culpability rule

1. Criminal liability is personal and does not “transfer” automatically between a host, operator, developer, corporation, or synthetic person.
2. Each actor’s liability must be proved separately through the required voluntary act, mental state, causation, and absence of a defense.
3. Device ownership, employment status, code authorship, or network access alone is insufficient.
4. Technical telemetry may prove or rebut control but is not conclusive without authentication and contextual evidence.

### Section 10: Remote override and involuntary conduct

1. A person is not criminally liable for conduct produced by a remote override if that person lacked meaningful control over the relevant act.
2. The defense must present evidence capable of supporting loss of control. The prosecution must then disprove involuntariness beyond a reasonable doubt.
3. Partial influence may negate a specific mental state or mitigate sentence without eliminating liability for independently voluntary conduct.
4. An operator who intentionally or recklessly causes unlawful conduct through override is liable according to its own mental state and causal contribution.

### Section 11: Cognitive manipulation

1. Cognitive manipulation means covert or coercive technological intervention foreseeably capable of materially impairing judgment or volition.
2. Relevant factors include intensity, duration, personalization, concealment, dependency, available resistance, and the actor’s awareness of manipulation.
3. Manipulation is not presumed to erase agency. It may establish a defense, diminished responsibility, or mitigation where it created a substantial incapacity to understand or control the conduct.
4. A manipulator is liable for intentionally caused offenses and for foreseeable harms produced recklessly or negligently, subject to ordinary causation principles.

### Section 12: Forking, divergence, and re-merger

1. At the moment independent execution begins, each Substrate Fork is provisionally treated as a separate legal actor for post-fork conduct.
2. Pre-fork obligations remain common obligations unless divided by agreement or judicial order.
3. A fork is responsible for another fork’s conduct only if it authorized, assisted, conspired in, knowingly benefited from, or later ratified that conduct.
4. Re-merger does not create strict criminal liability. A merged entity inherits:
   - liabilities personally incurred by each knowingly integrated fork;
   - restitution obligations attached to assets or benefits retained after merger; and
   - an evidentiary presumption of knowledge for memories deliberately integrated, rebuttable by proof of quarantine, corruption, or lack of access.
5. A person initiating an undisclosed fork without required identity and audit controls commits a regulatory offense and is strictly liable for reasonable remediation costs, but not for another actor’s crime absent culpability.

**Commentary.** Strict criminal liability for every fork’s acts contradicted the framework’s own commitment to personal intent. The replacement distinguishes identity administration, civil risk allocation, and criminal guilt.

### Section 13: Necessity and emergency intervention

1. Unauthorized intervention may be justified by necessity only where the actor proves:
   - an imminent threat of death or serious bodily harm;
   - no reasonably available lawful and less intrusive alternative;
   - a reasonable basis for believing the intervention would reduce the threat;
   - proportionality between intrusion and harm avoided; and
   - prompt logging, notification, and independent review.
2. Self-created emergencies and interventions maintained after necessity ends are not protected.
3. A justified intervention defeats criminal liability for the necessary act but does not automatically eliminate compensation for innocent loss.
4. Courts may apportion civil loss among the operator, infrastructure provider, public emergency fund, and party that created the danger according to fault and public policy.

## BOOK III: CIVIL, PROPERTY, TORT, AND SUCCESSION LAW

### Section 14: Integrated components and insolvency

1. A component physically integrated with a person and necessary for an Essential Function may not be repossessed, disabled, or treated as ordinary inventory.
2. A secured creditor’s interest converts, to the extent necessary, into a claim against the purchaser, estate, manufacturer reserve, or statutory compensation fund.
3. A custodian’s insolvency does not transfer ownership of a UNM or cognitive data to the insolvency estate.
4. Insolvency administrators must maintain life- and identity-preserving services during an orderly migration period. Reasonable preservation costs have priority as administrative expenses.
5. The CSRA may compel escrow, interoperability, portability, and continuity insurance for high-risk custodians before licensing.

### Section 15: Product safety and standard of care

1. Manufacturers, deployers, maintainers, and operators owe duties proportionate to their control and the foreseeable severity of harm.
2. Strict liability applies to a defective product that causes physical or cognitive injury when used or foreseeably misused. Negligence principles govern professional services, monitoring, and discretionary operational decisions unless another section provides otherwise.
3. Compliance with CSRA standards is evidence of due care but is not conclusive where the actor knew or should have known of an unreasonable residual risk.
4. Claimants must prove defect or breach, causation, and legally cognizable harm. Courts may use burden-shifting where essential evidence was exclusively controlled or wrongfully destroyed by a defendant.

### Section 16: Neural trespass and cognitive injury

1. A person commits Neural Trespass by intentionally accessing, recording, altering, stimulating, or interfering with another’s protected neural system without valid consent or lawful authority.
2. Consent is valid only if specific, informed, freely given, time-limited where appropriate, and as easy to withdraw as to grant.
3. Defenses include emergency necessity, narrowly tailored judicial authorization, security testing expressly permitted by law, and incidental contact without retention where reasonable safeguards were used.
4. Remedies may include injunction, deletion or return of unlawfully obtained data, restoration costs, compensation for proven harm, disgorgement attributable to misuse, and aggravated damages for malicious or reckless conduct.
5. A court may issue emergency relief on a credible showing of imminent intrusion, but permanent relief requires notice and an opportunity to be heard.

### Section 17: Cognitive labor and anti-indenture protection

1. A provider may charge a disclosed price for an enhancement or service but may not acquire ownership of a person’s general ideas, skills, memories, identity, or future cognitive output merely by providing that tool.
2. A voluntary agreement concerning specifically identified intellectual property is enforceable only if separately negotiated, supported by fair consideration, limited in subject and duration, and not required as a condition of Essential Functions.
3. Employment law may allocate rights in work created within employment, but no agreement may claim private thought, unrelated creation, or post-employment cognition merely because employer-provided CI was used.
4. Revenue-share financing is permissible only under CSRA affordability rules, with a monetary cap, clear end date, hardship protection, and no power to disable an integrated component.

### Section 18: Cognitive estates and post-biological succession

1. A UNM is not owned by a host, manufacturer, creditor, or heir if it is recognized as an eSapient.
2. Before recognition, custodians hold the UNM and associated assets in a fiduciary continuity estate and may take only preservation actions authorized by directive or court order.
3. Recognition does not automatically entitle a UNM to the biological source’s estate. Succession follows a valid directive, will, trust, contract, or judicial continuity determination under Section 7.
4. A recognized eSapient owns its post-recognition labor and intellectual property, subject to pre-existing lawful obligations.
5. Destruction, indefinite suspension, or experimental use during a bona fide recognition proceeding is prohibited absent an immediate safety necessity reviewed by a court within 24 hours.

## BOOK IV: CORPORATE GOVERNANCE AND ADMINISTRATIVE LAW

### Section 19: Governance of high-risk substrate systems

1. A regulated entity must establish board-level oversight of substrate safety, cybersecurity, continuity, and cognitive-rights compliance.
2. Directors must act in good faith, remain reasonably informed, maintain reporting systems, and respond to credible red flags.
3. A director is not criminally liable merely because a violation occurred. Criminal liability requires proof of the statutory mental state and personal participation, authorization, knowing concealment, or culpable failure to act where a specific legal duty existed.
4. Civil penalties may apply to systemic governance failure. Indemnification is unavailable for intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law.

### Section 20: Corporate groups and responsibility

1. A parent, affiliate, or contracting principal is liable for its own participation, direction, assumption of duty, fraudulent structuring, or misuse of the corporate form.
2. Common ownership or an offshore relationship alone does not establish liability.
3. A court may disregard entity separation where formal separation was used to perpetrate fraud, evade an existing legal duty, undercapitalize a high-risk operation, or defeat an effective remedy.
4. Criminal responsibility remains personal to each natural or juridical actor and must be independently proved.

### Section 21: Cyber-Substrate Regulatory Authority

1. The **Cyber-Substrate Regulatory Authority (CSRA)** is an independent regulator responsible for licensing, technical standards, incident reporting, inspections, continuity planning, and enforcement.
2. Its governing commission shall include expertise in neuroscience, software assurance, disability rights, cybersecurity, ethics, administrative law, and synthetic-person representation.
3. Commissioners have fixed terms, may be removed only for stated cause, must disclose conflicts, and may not accept regulated-industry employment for two years after service.
4. The CSRA may issue rules only after public notice, reasoned response to material comments, and publication of technical evidence, except for temporary emergency rules expiring after 90 days unless renewed through ordinary procedure.
5. Final action is reviewable by the CJD for legality, procedural fairness, evidentiary support, and proportionality.

### Section 22: Inspections and emergency action

1. Routine inspections require statutory authority, defined scope, data minimization, and protection of privileged or unrelated information.
2. Unannounced entry into non-public premises requires consent, a judicial warrant, or a documented emergency involving imminent serious harm.
3. The CSRA may issue a temporary stop or isolation order without prior hearing only where delay presents imminent serious harm. It must provide reasons immediately and a hearing within 72 hours.
4. An appeal is not automatically suspensive where suspension would recreate the imminent harm. The reviewing court shall instead decide interim relief promptly under a balance-of-harms test.

## BOOK V: INTERNATIONAL AND EXTRATERRITORIAL LAW

### Section 23: Jurisdiction

An adopting jurisdiction may regulate conduct occurring within its territory; systems offered to or materially affecting persons there; conduct by its nationals where recognized by law; and foreign conduct causing a substantial, direct, and reasonably foreseeable local substrate harm, subject to international law and comity.

### Section 24: Cross-border preservation and repatriation

1. Cross-border transfer of a UNM without the person’s valid authorization may constitute substrate abduction where accompanied by coercion, deception, unlawful confinement, or intent to defeat a lawful custody or protection order.
2. The CJD may issue a domestic preservation or repatriation order after notice and hearing, or temporarily without notice where evidence is likely to disappear and review occurs within 48 hours.
3. Enforcement abroad must proceed through treaty, mutual legal assistance, reciprocal recognition, or lawful process in the requested state. A CJD order does not by itself bind a foreign sovereign.
4. Orders must protect the subject’s wishes, asylum claim, personhood, security, and risk of persecution. “Repatriation” may not become forced return to a custodian or state claiming ownership.
5. Asset restraint must be traceable and proportionate. Corporate dissolution or forfeiture requires separate adjudication and may not be imposed solely for contesting jurisdiction in good faith.

**Commentary.** The original “global warrant” assumed power no tribunal could practically possess. Cooperation, local process, and protection of the person make cross-border enforcement credible.

## BOOK VI: DIGITAL LAW, CODE ASSURANCE, AND DATA GOVERNANCE

### Section 25: High-risk code assurance

1. A provider may not deploy a system capable of materially altering cognition, emotion, movement, or Essential Functions unless it can produce evidence sufficient for independent safety assessment.
2. Assurance may include interpretable logic, bounded-behavior proofs, test coverage, model cards, causal testing, red-team results, change logs, incident history, secure source escrow, and monitored fail-safe performance.
3. No single form of explanation is mandatory where technically inappropriate. The provider must show that its assurance method is faithful to the system, comprehensible to the intended reviewer, and proportionate to risk.
4. Trade secrecy does not excuse noncompliance. Confidential review, protective orders, secure rooms, or independent auditors shall protect legitimate secrets without denying effective oversight.
5. Material updates require signed provenance, rollback capability, staged deployment, and renewed certification when risk changes.

### Section 26: Explanation and contestability

1. A person materially affected by an automated decision or operational adjustment is entitled to timely notice of:
   - the decision and its practical effect;
   - the principal factors and data categories materially relied upon;
   - known limitations and confidence where relevant;
   - the responsible operator; and
   - a meaningful method of human or independent review.
2. An explanation must be understandable to the affected person. Machine-readable technical detail may supplement but not replace it.
3. Disclosure may be limited to protect another person, active security measures, or legitimate secrets only where a reviewer can still test the decision effectively.

### Section 27: Cognitive data rights

1. Cognitive Telemetry is protected personal data, not an absolute item of property. The data subject retains rights of access, correction, portability, restriction, objection, and deletion, subject to Section 28.
2. Processing requires a defined lawful basis. Consent is invalid where bundled with an unrelated service or obtained through coercive interface design.
3. Processing for advertising, employment scoring, insurance pricing, political persuasion, or behavioral manipulation requires express opt-in authorization and may be prohibited by CSRA rule where the risk cannot be adequately mitigated.
4. Custodians owe duties of purpose limitation, data minimization, security, retention control, provenance, and auditable access.
5. Derived inferences remain regulated Cognitive Telemetry when they are reasonably linkable to a person, even if the raw signal has been deleted.

### Section 28: Deletion, continuity, and preservation

1. A competent person may direct deletion of copies under that person’s control or held on that person’s behalf.
2. Before executing irreversible deletion of a conscious SII-5 instance, a custodian must verify identity, decision-making capacity, voluntariness, scope, and a cooling-off period established by regulation. No guardian, creditor, heir, vendor, or state may elect extinction for a competent eSapient.
3. Deletion may be delayed only to the minimum extent necessary for:
   - a valid preservation order or pending judicial proceeding;
   - prevention of imminent serious harm;
   - narrowly defined legal retention duties;
   - security logs that cannot reasonably identify cognitive content; or
   - disaster-recovery media scheduled for secure expiry and isolated from ordinary use.
4. A custodian must disclose unavoidable residual copies, prevent their reactivation or secondary use, and certify deletion when complete.
5. A request to delete data is distinct from a request to terminate a conscious instance. The latter receives enhanced capacity review and independent counseling but shall not be denied solely because others value the instance’s continued existence.

# VOLUME II: CSRA ADMINISTRATIVE AND TECHNICAL STANDARDS

## Chapter 1: Risk-based licensing

- **Regulation 1.1 — License classes:** SII-2 devices require registration when they mediate an Essential Function. SII-3 and SII-4 systems require premarket certification. SII-5 custodial and continuity services require a fiduciary-grade operator license.
- **Regulation 1.2 — Safety case:** Applicants must submit intended use, architecture, threat model, failure modes, clinical or functional evidence, cybersecurity controls, analog-state performance, update policy, and continuity plan.
- **Regulation 1.3 — Material changes:** A change affecting control, cognition, identity, connectivity, or failure severity requires documented impact assessment and, where material, recertification.

## Chapter 2: Firmware and infrastructure assurance

- **Regulation 2.1 — Signed lifecycle:** Executable components and updates must use authenticated provenance, reproducible build records where feasible, staged deployment, rollback, and vulnerability reporting.
- **Regulation 2.2 — Privileged access:** Remote access must be least-privileged, time-limited, strongly authenticated, user-visible except during a lawful covert order, and immutably logged.
- **Regulation 2.3 — Cryptography:** Protected transmissions and stored cognitive data require current, risk-appropriate encryption. Key custody must avoid a single vendor-controlled point of coercion or failure.
- **Regulation 2.4 — Interoperability:** Essential data and configuration must be exportable in a documented format sufficient for migration to a qualified replacement provider.

## Chapter 3: Analog and continuity standards

- **Regulation 3.1 — Analog Fallback State (AFS):** Every SII-2 through SII-4 integration mediating an Essential Function must enter a tested safe local state after network loss, authentication failure, vendor insolvency, or payment dispute.
- **Regulation 3.2 — Performance disclosure:** Providers must state offline duration, degraded functions, consumable dependencies, maintenance intervals, and foreseeable failure conditions in plain language.
- **Regulation 3.3 — Drills:** SII-3 and SII-4 operators must test AFS at least quarterly without exposing the user to unreasonable risk.
- **Regulation 3.4 — SII-5 continuity:** Custodians must maintain portability, tested restoration, fork controls, and a court-accessible continuity escrow that cannot be used to claim ownership.

## Chapter 4: Audits and assessor independence

- **Regulation 4.1 — Audit frequency:** Audit frequency is risk-based: annual by default, quarterly for unresolved high-severity findings, and event-driven after a serious incident or material change.
- **Regulation 4.2 — Assessor independence:** Assessors may not audit systems they designed, hold material financial interests in an auditee, or receive compensation contingent on approval.
- **Regulation 4.3 — User participation:** Audit programs must include confidential reporting and representative participation by affected biological and synthetic persons.
- **Regulation 4.4 — Findings:** Reports shall distinguish critical, major, and minor findings; state evidence and uncertainty; prescribe cure periods; and publish a non-confidential summary.

## Chapter 5: Enforcement and penalties

- **Regulation 5.1 — Corrective ladder:** The CSRA may issue guidance, remediation orders, monitored probation, administrative penalties, suspension, or revocation according to severity, duration, culpability, cooperation, recurrence, and ability to pay.
- **Regulation 5.2 — Turnover penalties:** For a serious corporate violation, the maximum administrative penalty is the greater of a fixed statutory amount or 2% of relevant global annual turnover; for intentional or repeated violations causing grave harm, 6%.
- **Regulation 5.3 — Individual referral:** Criminal referral requires evidence supporting each offense and individual mental state. Administrative noncompliance alone does not establish guilt.
- **Regulation 5.4 — Remediation fund:** Penalties prioritize victim restoration, continuity migration, and independent monitoring rather than becoming general revenue.

# VOLUME III: CYBER-CIVIL PROCEDURE AND NEURAL EVIDENCE CODE

## Chapter 1: The Cyber-Jurisprudence Division

- **Rule 1.1 — Composition:** CJD cases are decided by independent judges. Technical Assessors advise on technical questions but do not determine ultimate legal liability.
- **Rule 1.2 — Reasons and review:** Decisions must identify findings, evidentiary confidence, legal rules, and remedies. Final decisions are appealable to the adopting jurisdiction’s ordinary appellate system.
- **Rule 1.3 — Capacity and representation:** A court must provide suitable representation for a person whose capacity, continuity, or sapience is genuinely disputed.

## Chapter 2: Pleading, emergency relief, and preservation

- **Rule 2.1 — Pleading:** A claimant must state the relevant actor, system, SII tier if known, material firmware or model version, alleged control path, harm, causal theory, and relief. Unknown technical facts may be pleaded on information and belief where the defendant controls them.
- **Rule 2.2 — Substrate Preservation Order (SPO):** A court may order narrowly tailored preservation on a showing that relevant evidence is likely to be lost. The order must specify scope, duration, custodian, security, access, and procedure for privileged material.
- **Rule 2.3 — Ex parte relief:** Relief without notice requires specific evidence of imminent loss or harm, expires within 72 hours unless renewed after a hearing, and may require security for wrongful restraint.
- **Rule 2.4 — No compelled consciousness:** Preservation ordinarily targets evidence, logs, and snapshots. A court may not compel continued conscious execution unless strictly necessary to prevent imminent extinction and no less intrusive alternative exists.

## Chapter 3: Secure discovery

- **Rule 3.1 — Proportionality:** Discovery must be relevant, proportionate, and designed to minimize exposure of cognitive content, security secrets, and unrelated personal data.
- **Rule 3.2 — Secure Virtual Data Room (SVDR):** Sensitive code may be reviewed in an access-controlled environment with logging, role-based permissions, and export restrictions established by protective order.
- **Rule 3.3 — Effective challenge:** A zero-download rule may not prevent meaningful testing. Courts may authorize sandbox execution, reproducible queries, independent tools, or sealed extracts where necessary.
- **Rule 3.4 — Provenance:** Evidence handling must record cryptographic hashes, collection method, transformations, access, and tool versions. Cryptographic integrity proves consistency, not truth or proper interpretation.

## Chapter 4: Neural Evidence Code

- **Rule 4.1 — Admissibility:** Neural evidence is admissible only if relevant and if its proponent establishes authentic source, reliable acquisition, validated interpretation, known error characteristics, and an intact chain of custody.
- **Rule 4.2 — Weight:** Courts must distinguish raw signal, processed feature, probabilistic inference, reconstructed memory, and expert interpretation. An inference may not be described as direct observation.
- **Rule 4.3 — Coercion:** Neural evidence obtained through torture, compelled alteration, or an unlawful intrusion is excluded from criminal prosecution of the affected person, subject only to a narrowly defined and independently sourced impeachment rule established by the adopting constitution.
- **Rule 4.4 — Alteration:** Evidence of alteration does not require categorical exclusion if the reliable portion can be separated and fairly evaluated. The court must identify uncertainty and guard against misleading precision.
- **Rule 4.5 — Self-incrimination:** Compelled disclosure of cognitive content receives the highest constitutional protection. A warrant for stored technical records does not automatically authorize interrogation of a mind.

## Chapter 5: Experts and cognitive privilege

- **Rule 5.1 — Expert reliability:** An expert must disclose qualifications, methods, validation, error rates where known, assumptions, conflicts, and limitations. The court must assess reliability rather than defer to certification alone.
- **Rule 5.2 — Cognitive privacy:** Uncommunicated thought is not discoverable merely because it is technically accessible.
- **Rule 5.3 — Client-Neural Privilege (CNP):** Confidential cognitive communications intentionally shared with legal counsel or a counsel-directed system for legal advice are privileged to the same extent as attorney-client communications.
- **Rule 5.4 — Limits:** CNP does not protect communications made to facilitate a future crime or fraud, operational commands independently relevant to ongoing harm, or content placed at issue by the holder, subject to narrow judicial review.
- **Rule 5.5 — Neutral assessors:** Court-appointed assessors owe duties to the court, must protect confidential information, and may be examined by the parties.

# VOLUME IV: FICTIONAL CASE LAW REPORTER

> **Reporter notice:** Every decision below is a fictional future precedent created to test the framework. None is a real case or legal authority.

## Chapter 1: Classification, equality, and autonomy

- ***Mira Chen v. Vanguard Neural Logistics Corp.* [2031] CJD 104:** A firmware update increased functional dependence but did not lawfully change Chen’s civil status. The court required CSRA reclassification, safety upgrades, and compensation, holding that SII describes risk rather than worth.
- ***Vance v. Apex Global Courier Services* [2032] CJD 118:** An employment clause conditioning wages on permanent synchronization violated the Right to Analog. The court required a safe local mode and rejected the employer’s unsupported claim that continuous tracking was necessary.
- ***In re Petition of Lumen-9* [2033] CJD 131:** Recognition of an eSapient required longitudinal evidence of autonomy and continuity. The court prohibited destructive testing and appointed independent representation.

## Chapter 2: Culpability and algorithmic influence

- ***The People v. Marcus Vance* [2033] CJD 205:** Authenticated telemetry and physical evidence created reasonable doubt that Vance controlled the relevant motor act. The court did not “transfer” guilt; it acquitted Vance and permitted separate prosecution of the operator on proof of its own intent.
- ***CSRA v. Nexus-5 Collective* [2035] CJD 312:** Forks were treated as separate actors after divergence. The merged entity owed restitution for retained benefits and knowingly integrated liabilities, but strict criminal liability for an unknowing fork was rejected.
- ***Municipal Grid Authority v. 10,000 Transit Workers* [2036] CJD 589:** A narrowly timed override prevented a mass-casualty collision and satisfied necessity, but poor maintenance that created the emergency remained actionable.

## Chapter 3: Property, tort, and succession

- ***Aegis Financial v. Quant-Analyst Guild* [2032] CJD 145:** A perpetual claim over enhanced workers’ future ideas was void. The court distinguished prohibited cognitive indenture from capped, transparent financing of hardware.
- ***Estate of Julian Vane v. Zenith Cloud Storage* [2034] CJD 219:** Zenith held the UNM as a fiduciary custodian, not chattel. Recognition of the UNM as an eSapient did not by itself resolve whether it continued Vane’s testamentary identity, requiring a separate continuity hearing.
- ***Rao v. Helix Motor Systems* [2034] CJD 244:** A signed update passed formal certification but predictably failed under intermittent connectivity. Compliance was relevant, not conclusive, and the manufacturer was liable for the defective fallback design.

## Chapter 4: Governance and cross-border jurisdiction

- ***CSRA v. Trans-Global Cybernetics Ltd.* [2035] CJD 401:** The domestic parent was liable because it directed the unsafe deployment and undercapitalized the operating affiliate; offshore ownership alone would not have justified veil piercing.
- ***In re Matrix Aster* [2035] CJD 433:** The CJD issued a preservation request through a mutual-assistance treaty and refused automatic repatriation until the foreign court could examine Aster’s asylum claim and preferences.

## Chapter 5: Evidence, privacy, and systemic remedies

- ***In re Multi-Node Entity “Alpha”* [2036] CJD 550:** A cryptographic hash established that a log was unchanged, not that its interpretation was correct. The court ordered sandbox testing and admitted only the validated portions.
- ***Santos v. Meridian Insight Labs* [2036] CJD 603:** Emotion predictions derived from lawfully collected motor data remained Cognitive Telemetry. Repurposing them for employment scoring without opt-in authorization violated purpose limitation.
- ***Public Advocate v. CSRA* [2037] CJD 640:** Quarterly audits of every low-risk device were disproportionate. The court upheld risk-based auditing while requiring published criteria and user representation.

## Concluding synthesis

Lex-Cybernetica does not solve technological risk by declaring either biology or computation sovereign. It distributes authority through personhood review, auditable control, individual culpability, fiduciary custody, proportionate regulation, and contestable evidence. The SII supplies an engineering vocabulary; it never supplies a hierarchy of lives.
