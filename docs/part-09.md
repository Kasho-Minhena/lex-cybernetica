# PART IX — PERSON-ESSENTIAL SUBSTRATE, PROPERTY AND INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY

## Article 41 — Person-essential substrate

1. **Person-essential substrate** means hardware, software, tissue, energy, cooling, network, authentication, storage or other infrastructure whose loss would, in the actual configuration, destroy, imprison, materially disable or irreversibly impair a Person or a Personhood claimant protected under Article 8.
2. Ordinary property status does not end the legal analysis where exercise of property rights would have such an effect.
3. A party seeking repossession, shutdown, disposal or reconfiguration of Person-essential substrate MUST identify the effect on the Person and any reasonably available transition.
4. A competent authority MAY impose temporary preservation or transition measures, subject to proportionality, cost allocation and review.

## Article 42 — No ownership of a Person

1. A Person MUST NOT be owned.
2. Creation, financing, training, gestation, hosting, design, cloning, copying, maintenance, rescue or supply of intellectual property does not create ownership of the Person.
3. A contract, security interest, licence, employment arrangement, guardianship or financing instrument is unenforceable to the extent that it purports to make the Person collateral, transferable property or compulsory labour.
4. Legitimate claims concerning money, equipment, intellectual property or services remain enforceable against the appropriate contracting party or property, subject to this Article.

## Article 43 — Separable creator and provider rights

1. Copyright, patent, trade-secret, contractual, hardware and service rights MAY remain property even where they contributed to a Person's Genesis or continued functioning.
2. Such rights MUST be identified separately from Personhood and from ownership of the Person.
3. A right or asset is **legally separable** for this Part only to the extent that it can be recognised or enforced without treating the Person as property, compelling the Person's labour or presence, or causing unlawful destruction, detention or Material Control of the Person.
4. Technical inseparability does not erase legitimate IP or property rights; it requires the conflict to be resolved under Articles 44 and 45 rather than by pretending the Person and the protected asset are the same legal object.
5. A creator does not acquire a claim to the Person's future labour, presence, cognition, relationships or outputs merely because protected IP contributed to the Person's architecture.
6. A Person does not automatically acquire ownership of all creator IP merely because it is necessary to the Person's functioning.

## Article 44 — IP enforcement affecting Essential Functions

1. A court or competent authority considering an IP remedy that would foreseeably disable an Essential Function MUST consider:
   a. seriousness of infringement;
   b. availability of monetary or narrower relief;
   c. risk to life, continuity and autonomy;
   d. availability and timing of substitute technology;
   e. security and trade-secret risks;
   f. whether the dependency was knowingly created.
2. A patent injunction, licence termination or trade-secret restriction MUST NOT operate as a de facto ownership or destruction right over a Person where a less harmful effective remedy is reasonably available.
3. Necessary technical disclosure MAY be ordered on a targeted, secure and confidential basis.
4. Nothing in this Article creates universal source-code disclosure. A pattern of successive targeted disclosure orders that, in aggregate, would functionally amount to universal disclosure MUST be assessed cumulatively by the forum, which MAY consolidate, limit or decline further disclosure to the extent necessary to avoid circumvention of this paragraph while preserving effective adjudication.

## Article 45 — Creditors, continuity scarcity and competing essential claims

1. Creditor priority, contract rank or Personhood alone MUST NOT automatically decide allocation where genuinely scarce resources cannot preserve every affected Essential Function.
2. A competent authority MUST consider:
   a. immediacy and severity of harm;
   b. probability that the resource will preserve the function;
   c. availability of substitutes;
   d. duration of required support;
   e. prior dedicated continuity arrangements;
   f. effects on other Persons;
   g. whether scarcity was engineered or avoidable.
3. Temporary preservation MAY override ordinary possession or shutdown rights only to the extent authorised by law and subject to fair cost allocation where feasible.
4. A Person MUST NOT be treated as having unlimited priority over all property or public resources merely by virtue of Personhood.
5. Deliberate creation of artificial scarcity to force surrender of rights is unlawful.
6. Where the factors in paragraph 2 remain materially in equipoise, the authority SHOULD prefer the allocation that minimises expected irreversible loss of life or continuity across affected Persons and claimants, and then the allocation least dependent on engineered or reasonably avoidable scarcity. The authority MUST state its reasons.
