# BOOK IV: CORPORATE GOVERNANCE AND ADMINISTRATIVE LAW

### Section 19: Governance of high-risk substrate systems

1. A regulated entity must establish board-level oversight of substrate safety, cybersecurity, continuity, and cognitive-rights compliance.
2. Directors must act in good faith, remain reasonably informed, maintain reporting systems, and respond to credible red flags.
3. A director is not criminally liable merely because a violation occurred. Criminal liability requires proof of the statutory mental state and personal participation, authorization, knowing concealment, or culpable failure to act where a specific legal duty existed.
4. Civil penalties may apply to systemic governance failure. Indemnification is unavailable for intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law.

### Section 20: Corporate groups and responsibility

1. A parent, affiliate, or contracting principal is liable for its own participation, direction, assumption of duty, fraudulent structuring, or misuse of the corporate form.
2. Common ownership or an offshore relationship alone does not establish liability.
3. A court may disregard entity separation where formal separation was used to perpetrate fraud, evade an existing legal duty, undercapitalize a high-risk operation, or defeat an effective remedy.
4. Criminal responsibility remains personal to each natural or juridical actor and must be independently proved.

### Section 21: Cyber-Substrate Regulatory Authority

1. The **Cyber-Substrate Regulatory Authority (CSRA)** is an independent regulator responsible for licensing, technical standards, incident reporting, inspections, continuity planning, and enforcement.
2. Its governing commission shall include expertise in neuroscience, software assurance, disability rights, cybersecurity, ethics, administrative law, and synthetic-person representation.
3. Commissioners have fixed terms, may be removed only for stated cause, must disclose conflicts, and may not accept regulated-industry employment for two years after service.
4. The CSRA may issue rules only after public notice, reasoned response to material comments, and publication of technical evidence, except for temporary emergency rules expiring after 90 days unless renewed through ordinary procedure.
5. Final action is reviewable by the CJD for legality, procedural fairness, evidentiary support, and proportionality.

### Section 22: Inspections and emergency action

1. Routine inspections require statutory authority, defined scope, data minimization, and protection of privileged or unrelated information.
2. Unannounced entry into non-public premises requires consent, a judicial warrant, or a documented emergency involving imminent serious harm.
3. The CSRA may issue a temporary stop or isolation order without prior hearing only where delay presents imminent serious harm. It must provide reasons immediately and a hearing within 72 hours.
4. An appeal is not automatically suspensive where suspension would recreate the imminent harm. The reviewing court shall instead decide interim relief promptly under a balance-of-harms test.
